Gridded Ion Engine II: Acceleration

Electric Propulsion

Now that most of the neutral propellant particles have been ionized, they are ready to be accelerated to produce useful thrust $F_N$! A large voltage difference is applied over the short distance between the screen and accelerator grids, creating a strong electric field $E = V_T/d$. Much like chemical rockets, only so many particles can be accelerated at a time via the accelerator grid. This means that for a given gridded ion engine size, there is a limit to the amount of thrust that can be generated. Let’s explore what the limit seems to be.1

Guiding Question

The more ions that pass through the accelerator grids, the greater our mass flow rate and thus our thrust $F_N$. What causes the fundamental limit on the number of ions that pass through the screen grid into the accelerator grid?

Guiding Question

Just downstream of the accelerator grid, electrons from the neutralizer join the exhaust jet to neutralize it. These electrons are attracted to the ions coming out of the accelerator grid openings. Why might we want to implement a small voltage rise just downstream of the accelerator grid, knowing this would lower thrust $F_N$ and $I_{sp}$?

Child–Langmuir Space Charge
Conceptual Questions
If we have both positively charged ions and negatively charged electrons available to us, why do we always choose to accelerate the ions and not the electrons?
Recall that electrons have far less mass than ions, by about five orders of magnitude for xenon. While they can be accelerated extremely fast, their minuscule mass means that the thrust $F_N$ an electron jet produces is near zero.
The larger $V_T/d$ (the electric field $E$ between the screen and accelerating grids) is, the higher thrust density $F/A$ we can achieve. Why can’t $V_T/d$ be arbitrarily high?
The desired mission and thus desired exhaust velocity $v_{ex}$ or specific impulse set $V_{net}$, the voltage the ion actually keeps once it’s slowed back down past the decelerator grid, as related by $v_{ex} = \sqrt{2qV_{net}/m}$. $V_T$, the full screen–accelerator gap voltage, must be at least as large as $V_{net}$, so a mission’s exhaust-velocity requirement puts a floor under $V_T$ too. Beyond that floor, to maximize $V_T/d$, we want to minimize $d$, the distance between the screen and accelerator grids. However, if $d$ is too small, then the risk of the screen–accelerator grid circuit shorting is high. This is especially true after vibrations during launch, as grids might shift, causing a short. Additionally, recall that $V_T/d$ is the electric field $E$ strength between the screen and accelerator grids. If $E$-field is too strong, then breakdown can occur: creating electrical arcs between the two grids, damaging them.
Child–Langmuir Law

The current density $j$ in between the screen and accelerator grid is limited by the Child–Langmuir Law.3 If too many positively charged ions exist between the two grids, the next ion to be extracted by the grid field $E$ would be repelled by the amount of ions in between the grids.2

Space-Charge-Limited Current Density3,5 $$ \boxed{\;\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j} = \frac{4}{9}\,\varepsilon_0 \sqrt{\frac{2q}{m}}\, \frac{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}^{3/2}}{d^{2}}\;} $$
Show derivation
Poisson’s Equation $$ \nabla^{2}\phi = \frac{d^{2}\phi}{dx^{2}} = -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} $$ Current Density $$ \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j} = \rho v \;\;\rightarrow\;\; \rho = \frac{\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}}{v} $$ Conservation of Energy $$ \tfrac{1}{2}m v_0^{2} + q\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T} = \tfrac{1}{2}m v^{2} + q\phi $$ $$ \rightarrow\; v = \sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}\left(\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi\right)} \;\;\rightarrow\;\; \rho = \frac{\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}}{\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}\left(\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi\right)}} $$ Substitute into Poisson’s Equation $$ \frac{d^{2}\phi}{dx^{2}} = -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} = -\frac{\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}}{\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}}}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi}} $$ Recall Chain + Power Rule $$ \frac{d}{dx}\!\left(\frac{d\phi}{dx}\right)^{2} = 2\,\frac{d\phi}{dx}\,\frac{d^{2}\phi}{dx^{2}} $$ $$ \rightarrow\; \frac{d}{dx}\!\left(\frac{d\phi}{dx}\right)^{2} = \frac{-2\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}\,\frac{d\phi}{dx}}{\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}}\,\sqrt{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi}} $$ $$ \rightarrow\; \int_{0}^{\frac{d\phi}{dx}} d\!\left(\frac{d\phi}{dx}\right)^{2} = \frac{-2\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}}{\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}}} \int_{V_T}^{\phi}\frac{d\phi'}{\sqrt{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi'}} $$

The field vanishes at the screen grid at space-charge saturation, so $\left.\tfrac{d\phi}{dx}\right|_{0} = 0$ with $\phi(0)=\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}$.

$$ \rightarrow\; \left(\frac{d\phi}{dx}\right)^{2} = \frac{-2\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}}{\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}}}\,(-2)\left(\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi\right)^{1/2} $$

$\phi$ falls monotonically from $\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}$ at the screen grid to $0$ at the accelerator grid, so $d\phi/dx \le 0$ across the whole gap — take the negative root:

$$ \rightarrow\; \frac{d\phi}{dx} = -\sqrt{\frac{4\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}}{\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}}}} \left(\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi\right)^{1/4} $$ Separate & Integrate $$ \int_{V_T}^{\phi}\frac{d\phi'}{\left(\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi'\right)^{1/4}} = -\sqrt{\frac{4\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}}{\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}}}}\int_{0}^{x}dx' $$ $$ \rightarrow\; -\frac{4}{3}\left(\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi\right)^{3/4}\Big|_{V_T}^{\phi} = -\sqrt{\frac{4\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}}{\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}}}}\;x $$ $$ \rightarrow\; \frac{4}{3}\left(\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi\right)^{3/4} = \sqrt{\frac{4\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}}{\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}}}}\;x $$ Square Both Sides $$ \frac{16}{9}\left(\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi\right)^{3/2} = \frac{4\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}}{\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}}}\,x^{2} $$ Rearrange for Current $$ \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j} = \frac{4}{9}\,\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}}\, \frac{\left(\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi\right)^{3/2}}{x^{2}} $$ Evaluate at $x=d,\;\phi=0$ $$ \boxed{\;\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j} = \frac{4}{9}\,\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}}\, \frac{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}^{3/2}}{d^{2}}\;} $$
Thrust Density

The thrust density $F/A$ is important because it helps us determine the approximate thrust $F_N$ of our device and approximate how well the device would perform at different sizes. For example, a large thrust density $F/A$ is optimal for a miniature thruster, as these thrusters have smaller exit areas.

Maximum Thrust Density4 $$ \boxed{\;\frac{\textcolor{#FF4400}{F}}{A} = \frac{8}{9}\,\varepsilon_0\, \frac{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}^{3/2}\sqrt{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_{net}}}}{d^{2}} = \frac{8}{9}\,\varepsilon_0 \left(\frac{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}}{d}\right)^{2}\sqrt{\textcolor{#F5C518}{R}}\;} \qquad \textcolor{#F5C518}{R} \equiv \textcolor{#F5C518}{V_{net}}/\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T} $$
Show derivation
Maxwell Stress Tensor $$ T_{ij} = \sum \left(\varepsilon_0 E_i E_j - \tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 \delta_{ij} E_k E_k\right) $$ Isolate $x$-Component $$ T_{xx} = \varepsilon_0 E_x^{2} - \tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0\left(E_x^{2}+E_y^{2}+E_z^{2}\right) $$ Electric Pressure $$ T = \tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 \textcolor{#F5C518}{E}^{2} \;\;\rightarrow\;\; \frac{\textcolor{#FF4400}{F}}{A} = \tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 \textcolor{#F5C518}{E}^{2} $$ Potential at Space-Charge Saturation $$ \frac{\left(\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi\right)^{3/2}}{x^{2}} = \frac{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}^{3/2}}{d^{2}} \;\;\rightarrow\;\; \left(1-\frac{\phi}{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}}\right)^{3/2} = \left(\frac{x}{d}\right)^{2} $$ $$ \rightarrow\; 1-\frac{\phi}{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}} = \left(\frac{x}{d}\right)^{4/3} \;\;\rightarrow\;\; \phi = \textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}\left(1-\left(\frac{x}{d}\right)^{4/3}\right) $$ Recall $E = -\,d\phi/dx$ $$ \textcolor{#F5C518}{E} = -\frac{d\phi}{dx} = \frac{4}{3}\left(\frac{x}{d}\right)^{1/3}\frac{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}}{d} $$ Substitute into Electric Pressure (at $x=d$) $$ \frac{\textcolor{#FF4400}{F}}{A} = \tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 \textcolor{#F5C518}{E}^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0\left(\frac{4}{3}\frac{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}}{d}\right)^{2} $$ $$ \rightarrow\; \frac{\textcolor{#FF4400}{F}}{A} = \frac{8}{9}\,\varepsilon_0 \left(\frac{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}}{d}\right)^{2} = \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}\,\frac{m}{q}\sqrt{\frac{2q\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}}{m}} $$

That last form is the momentum flux at the accelerator grid, using the velocity the ion has there, $\sqrt{2q\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}/m}$. But past the decelerator grid the ion is slowed back down toward spacecraft potential, so it actually leaves with $\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_{ex}}=\sqrt{2q\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_{net}}/m}$ instead. The particle flux $\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}$ doesn’t change downstream — only the velocity does — so the thrust density actually delivered to the spacecraft swaps that one velocity factor, $V_T \rightarrow V_{net}$:

Swap in the Velocity the Ion Actually Leaves With $$ \boxed{\;\frac{\textcolor{#FF4400}{F}}{A} = \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}\,\frac{m}{q} \sqrt{\frac{2q\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_{net}}}{m}} = \frac{8}{9}\,\varepsilon_0\, \frac{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}^{3/2}\sqrt{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_{net}}}}{d^{2}} = \frac{8}{9}\,\varepsilon_0 \left(\frac{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}}{d}\right)^{2}\sqrt{\textcolor{#F5C518}{R}}\;} \qquad \textcolor{#F5C518}{R} \equiv \textcolor{#F5C518}{V_{net}}/\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T} $$
