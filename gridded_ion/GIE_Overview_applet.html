Gridded Ion Engine: Overview

Electric Propulsion

Before we look into the physics, it is important to acknowledge the history under which electrostatic thrusters were developed. Nearly all electric propulsion devices used today are electrostatic devices, meaning they use solely electric fields to accelerate charged particles for thrust. During the Cold War, the United States and Soviet Union began research on two such electrostatic thrusters: the gridded ion engine and the Hall-Effect thruster. While the United States began early theoretical research on the Hall-Effect thruster, its intrinsic plasma instabilities dissuaded further development efforts. The United States would go on to focus on the development of the gridded ion engine, while the Soviet Union would continue to research the Hall-Effect thruster.

Gridded ion engines are highly efficient electric propulsion devices used on modern satellite platforms and several high profile missions. These ion engines have three characteristic features: an ionization chamber, accelerator grids, and a neutralizer. While each of these features varies from thruster to thruster, each variation serves the same purpose.

Governing Equations

Every gridded ion engine is the same short chain of relations, one stage handing off to the next. The ionization chamber decides what fraction of the propellant becomes ions; the grids set the exhaust velocity and cap the current they can pass; the neutralizer keeps the charge books balanced. Read them once here, then watch each stage build its own.

Thrust Thrust is momentum flux — the mass flow rate leaving the engine times the speed it leaves at. Nothing about the thruster being electric changes this. $$ \boxed{\;\textcolor{#FF4400}{F} = \textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot m}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c}\;} \qquad \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{I_{sp}} = \frac{\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c}}{g_0} $$
Exhaust Velocity An ion falling through the net voltage converts all of it into kinetic energy. This is the one equation that makes the engine electrostatic: pick a voltage, and you have picked an exhaust velocity. $$ \textcolor{#C060FF}{q}\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_{net}} = \tfrac12\,\textcolor{#FF4400}{m}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c}^{2} \;\;\rightarrow\;\; \boxed{\;\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c} = \sqrt{\frac{2\,\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}\,\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_{net}}} {\textcolor{#FF4400}{m}}}\;} $$ Heavier propellant is slower at the same volts — xenon is chosen precisely because a large $m$ buys thrust per unit of power.
Propellant Utilization The fraction of injected propellant that actually leaves as ions rather than escaping neutral. Everything the magnetic cusps do is in service of this one number. $$ \textcolor{#C060FF}{\mu_u} = \frac{\textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot m_i}}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot m}} $$
Electrostatic Field The grids do not push on the ions directly; they impose a potential, and its slope is the force per unit charge. Across a gap $d$ held at $V_T$, that slope is just $V_T/d$. $$ \textcolor{#F5C518}{\vec E} = -\nabla\textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi} \;\;\xrightarrow{\;\text{1-D gap}\;}\;\; \textcolor{#F5C518}{E} = -\frac{d\textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi}}{dx} \;\;\rightarrow\;\; \frac{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}}{d} $$
Space-Charge Limit — Child–Langmuir The ions already in the gap carry their own charge, which flattens the very potential pulling the next ones in. Solving Poisson’s equation for that self-consistent field caps the current density the gap can pass — and with it the thrust density. Derived on the acceleration page. $$ \boxed{\;\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j} = \frac{4}{9}\,\varepsilon_0 \sqrt{\frac{2\,\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m}}}\, \frac{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}^{3/2}}{d^{2}}\;} \qquad \boxed{\;\frac{\textcolor{#FF4400}{F}}{A} = \frac{8}{9}\,\varepsilon_0 \left(\frac{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}}{d}\right)^{2}\sqrt{\textcolor{#F5C518}{R}}\;} $$ Note the $1/d^{2}$: closing the grids buys current far faster than raising the voltage does. $R = V_{net}/V_T$ is the fraction of the total voltage the ion keeps after the accelerator grid decelerates it back on the way out.
Charge Balance The beam is a current of ions, so the neutralizer must emit exactly one electron per ion. Fail this and the spacecraft charges negative until it drags its own beam back. $$ \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{I_b} = \frac{\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}\,\textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot m_i}}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m}} = \textcolor{#ffe94d}{I_e} \qquad \textcolor{#FF4400}{F} = \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{I_b} \sqrt{\frac{2\,\textcolor{#FF4400}{m}\,\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_{net}}}{\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}}} $$ That last form is just $F = \dot m c$ rewritten in the quantities an engineer actually sets on the power supplies — beam current and voltage. Each stage below takes one of these apart.
Ionization

The first stage of the gridded ion engine involves the ionization chamber, which ionizes neutral propellant atoms so that they can be propelled by the upcoming accelerator grids. Because ionization requires the electrons to have a certain amount of energy, ionization chambers are equipped with magnetic cusps that lengthen the path an electron travels before being collected by the inside walls of the thruster. This way, a given electron has more opportunities to ionize neutral propellant particles before it leaves the chamber.

Acceleration

The second stage of the gridded ion engine provides the thrust of the engine through a strong electric field between accelerating grids. A voltage difference is applied between a screen grid and an accelerating grid placed a small distance apart, creating a strong electric field that accelerates incoming ions. However, there exists a limit on how many ions can be between the screen and accelerating grid: the more ions that exist there, the more incoming ions are repelled away.

Neutralization

The third stage of the gridded ion engine neutralizes the ion beam expelled from the accelerating grids by emitting electrons that join the beam, creating an overall neutral jet of ions and electrons: a plasma jet. If the ion beam were not neutralized, the spacecraft would be left negatively charged, since all the positive charge has left via the jet.

Conceptual Questions
Gridded ion engines have very high specific impulses, in the order of several thousands of seconds, permitting them to be used on very long missions. However, missions are often more limited by their thruster components’ lifetimes rather than the amount of propellant available to them. What component is generally the most limiting to the lifetime of the thruster?
The accelerating grid is often the most lifetime-limiting component in a gridded ion engine. By the time ions reach the accelerating grid, they have reached their maximum speed. If they collide with the grid instead of passing through the gaps, they “sputter”, eroding the grid material. Additionally, ionization can occur past the accelerating grids. At this point, the newly created ion is moving slowly and is highly attracted to the negatively charged accelerator grid, causing erosion. Most gridded ion engines implement a “decelerator grid”, which is neutral. Ions that come back toward the accelerator grid thus hit the decelerator instead, extending the operating lifetime of the accelerator grid.
