The Plasma Sheath

Plasma Physics

Every plasma we care about is in a box. It touches a discharge-chamber wall, an accelerator grid, a Hall thruster channel, a Langmuir probe, a cathode keeper. The bulk of that plasma is quasineutral, but the boundary cannot be, and the thin non-neutral layer that forms there is called the sheath. Almost everything a plasma does to its container happens inside it: the wall losses that eat into efficiency, the ion energies that sputter away ceramic, the current a probe collects, the field that pulls a beam out of a grid.

The reason a sheath exists at all is a mismatch in speed. At the same temperature an electron moves hundreds of times faster than a xenon ion, simply because it is hundreds of thousands of times lighter. So a fresh, uncharged wall is bombarded by far more electrons than ions, and it charges negative. That negative potential $\phi$ then repels the very electrons that created it, until exactly as many arrive as ions do. The wall has found its floating potential, and the plasma has grown a sheath.

Guiding Question

An electrically isolated wall is dropped into a quasineutral plasma. After it settles, what is the net electrical current flowing into it?

Demo 1: Why the Wall Goes Negative

The wall starts at the plasma potential, where the electron flux is more than three hundred times the ion flux. Watch the two pins on the logarithmic axis. As the wall charges down, more and more electrons are turned back at the sheath edge and the electron pin slides left until the two meet. Change the ion species: a lighter ion moves faster, closes the gap on its own, and needs a shallower well to balance it. Then uncheck floating and bias the wall by hand. That is a Langmuir probe, and the floating potential is simply where its current-voltage curve crosses zero.

Both fluxes can be written down. The electrons are held back by the wall, so only the fast tail of the distribution gets through; because they are in thermal equilibrium in the retarding field, their density follows the Boltzmann relation and their flux is the ordinary one-sided random flux multiplied by an exponential factor. The ions are accelerated by the wall, so their flux is not thermal at all: it is set by the speed at which they arrive at the sheath edge, which we come back to in the next section.

The Floating Potential

Setting the two fluxes equal and solving for the potential $\phi$ gives a result that depends on nothing but the electron temperature and the ion-to-electron mass ratio:

Floating Potential $$ \boxed{\; \textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi_w} - \textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi_p} \;=\; -\,\frac{kT_e}{2\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}} \left[\, \ln\!\left(\frac{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_i}}{2\pi \textcolor{#FF4400}{m_e}}\right) + 1 \right] \;} $$ for xenon this is $-5.77\,kT_e/\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}$  ·  argon $-5.18$  ·  hydrogen $-3.34$
Show derivation
Electron Flux to the Wall In the retarding field of the sheath the electrons stay Boltzmann-distributed, $\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_e} = \textcolor{#C060FF}{n_s}\,e^{\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi}/kT_e}$, measuring $\textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi}$ from the sheath edge. The one-sided random flux of a Maxwellian through a plane is $\tfrac14 n\bar{v}$, so $$ \textcolor{#C060FF}{\Gamma_e} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{4}\,\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_s}\,\bar{v}_e\, e^{\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi_w}/kT_e}, \qquad \bar{v}_e = \sqrt{\frac{8kT_e}{\pi \textcolor{#FF4400}{m_e}}} $$
Ion Flux to the Wall Ions are not repelled, so nothing suppresses them: every ion that reaches the sheath edge is collected. Their flux is therefore fixed by the density and speed there. As the next section shows, that speed is the Bohm speed $\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c_s}$, so $$ \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\Gamma_i} \;=\; \textcolor{#C060FF}{n_s}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c_s}, \qquad \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c_s} = \sqrt{\frac{kT_e}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_i}}} $$
Balance Them A floating wall carries no net current, so $\textcolor{#C060FF}{\Gamma_e} = \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\Gamma_i}$. The density $\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_s}$ cancels from both sides, so the answer will not depend on how dense the plasma is: $$ e^{\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi_w}/kT_e} \;=\; \frac{4\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c_s}}{\bar{v}_e} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{2\pi \textcolor{#FF4400}{m_e}}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_i}}} $$ Taking the logarithm gives the drop across the sheath itself, measured from the sheath edge: $$ \textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi_w} \;=\; -\,\frac{kT_e}{2\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}}\, \ln\!\left(\frac{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_i}}{2\pi \textcolor{#FF4400}{m_e}}\right) $$
Refer It Back to the Bulk Plasma The sheath edge is not at the plasma potential either. Getting the ions up to $\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c_s}$ costs another $kT_e/2\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}$ of potential drop across the pre-sheath, which is the $+1$ in the boxed result: $$ \boxed{\; \textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi_w} - \textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi_p} \;=\; -\,\frac{kT_e}{2\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}} \left[\, \ln\!\left(\frac{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_i}}{2\pi \textcolor{#FF4400}{m_e}}\right) + 1 \right] \;} $$
Heavier ions dig a deeper well. The mass ratio sits inside a logarithm, so the dependence is weak: going from hydrogen to xenon multiplies $\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_i}$ by 130 but only deepens the well from $3.3\,kT_e/\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}$ to $5.8\,kT_e/\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}$. The floating potential is a stubborn few times the electron temperature, essentially always.
Density does not appear. The floating potential is set by $T_e$ and the mass ratio alone. Density controls how thick the sheath is, through the Debye length, but not how deep it is.
A real wall also emits secondary electrons when ions and electrons strike it. Those leave the wall and count as a positive current, so they make the sheath shallower. In a Hall thruster with hot electrons and a boron-nitride channel the emission can approach unit yield, and the sheath collapses into a space-charge saturated state that no longer obeys the formula above.

That derivation quietly assumed something: that ions arrive at the sheath edge already moving at $\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c_s}$. Where would they get that speed, and why exactly that value?

Inside the sheath both species respond to the same potential $\phi$, but in opposite ways. The electrons are repelled, so their density falls off exponentially, as $e^{-\chi}$. The ions are accelerated, so by flux conservation their density falls off only as an inverse square root of the potential. For a sheath to exist, the ion density must stay above the electron density everywhere, and that net positive charge is what holds the potential down. Whether it does depends entirely on how fast the ions were going when they arrived.

The Bohm Criterion

Comparing the two rates of decay at the sheath edge gives a condition on the ion entry speed, first written down by David Bohm in 1949:

Bohm Criterion $$ \boxed{\; \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{u_s} \;\ge\; \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c_s} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{kT_e}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_i}}} \;} $$ the ion-acoustic speed. For xenon at $T_e = 5$ eV, $\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c_s} \approx 1900$ m/s
Show derivation
Set Up the Two Densities Write $\chi = -\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi}/kT_e$, so $\chi$ grows from zero at the sheath edge into the sheath. Electrons are Boltzmann; ions conserve energy and flux, entering with speed $\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{u_s}$: $$ \textcolor{#C060FF}{n_e} = \textcolor{#C060FF}{n_s}\,e^{-\chi}, \qquad \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{n_i} = \textcolor{#C060FF}{n_s} \left(1 + \frac{2\chi}{M^{2}}\right)^{-1/2}, \qquad M \equiv \frac{\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{u_s}}{\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c_s}} $$
Poisson's Equation Measuring distance in Debye lengths, $\xi = x/\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\lambda_D}$, Poisson's equation becomes $$ \frac{d^{2}\chi}{d\xi^{2}} \;=\; \left(1 + \frac{2\chi}{M^{2}}\right)^{-1/2} - e^{-\chi} $$
Linearize at the Sheath Edge Just inside the edge $\chi$ is small. Expanding both terms to first order, the constant parts cancel, which is quasineutrality, and what is left is $$ \frac{d^{2}\chi}{d\xi^{2}} \;=\; \left(1 - \frac{1}{M^{2}}\right)\chi $$
Read Off the Condition This is the equation of exponential growth if the bracket is positive and of a sine if it is negative. A sine would carry $\chi$ negative, meaning the potential $\phi$ rises above the plasma potential inside the sheath, a barrier that repels the ions the sheath is supposed to be collecting. Only the growing solution is physical, so $$ 1 - \frac{1}{M^{2}} \ge 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\;\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{u_s} \ge \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c_s} = \sqrt{kT_e/\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_i}}\;} $$
The plasma takes the equality. Nothing in the sheath forces $M$ to be any particular value above one, but the pre-sheath that supplies the ions cannot over-deliver: its own solution runs out at $M = 1$. Real plasmas therefore sit exactly at the marginal case, which is why $\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c_s}$ and not some larger speed appears in every wall-loss formula.
Notice which temperature appears. $\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c_s}$ is built from the electron temperature and the ion mass. The ions are usually cold (near room temperature in an ion thruster discharge), and yet they arrive at the wall moving at kilometers per second. They are not pushed there by their own pressure; they are pulled by the electrons' pressure through the ambipolar field.
The argument above treats the ions as a cold beam. Hutchinson gives the kinetic generalisation, in which the criterion becomes a statement about $\langle 1/v^{2}\rangle$ over the ion distribution. For the cold-ion plasmas of electric propulsion the two agree.
Guiding Question

Suppose ions drifted into the sheath edge more slowly than the Bohm speed. What goes wrong?

Demo 2: The Bohm Criterion

Drag the entry Mach number down through 1. Above it, the potential $\phi$ climbs away monotonically and the dashed non-linear solution runs off the top of the plot into the wall, a real sheath. Below it, the same equation gives a cosine that dives straight into the shaded forbidden region where φ sits above the plasma potential. There is no way to patch it: a sub-sonic ion stream simply cannot support a sheath. Sit exactly at $M = 1$ and the linear term vanishes altogether, and that knife-edge is where nature actually operates.

So the ions must already be supersonic when they reach the sheath. They are cold, and the sheath cannot reach out to accelerate them: it is only a few Debye lengths thick and its field is screened off beyond that. The acceleration therefore has to happen in the quasineutral plasma itself, over a much longer region called the pre-sheath. There the charge densities are still equal to a very good approximation, but not exactly equal, and the small residual field does the work. Its length is not set by the Debye length at all: it is set by whatever supplies the ions: the ionization mean free path, the ion-neutral collision length, or the size of the device, whichever is shortest.

The Pre-Sheath Drop

Whatever its length, the potential drop across the pre-sheath is pinned, because it has exactly one job: take the ions from rest to cs.

Pre-Sheath Drop $$ \boxed{\; \textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi_s} - \textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi_p} = -\frac{kT_e}{2\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}}, \qquad \textcolor{#C060FF}{n_s} = \textcolor{#C060FF}{n_0}\,e^{-1/2} \approx 0.61\,\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_0} \;} $$
Show derivation
Energy, for an Ion Born at Rest An ion created in the bulk falls through the pre-sheath potential. When it has reached $\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c_s}$, $$ \tfrac{1}{2}\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_i}\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c_s}^{2} = \textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\,|\Delta\textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi}| \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |\Delta\textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi}| = \frac{kT_e}{2\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}} $$ which is one half of an electron-volt per volt of electron temperature, or 2.5 V for a 5 eV discharge.
Density at the Sheath Edge The pre-sheath is quasineutral, so the electrons set the density everywhere in it through the Boltzmann relation, and the ions must follow: $$ \frac{\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_s}}{\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_0}} = e^{\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\Delta\textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi}/kT_e} = e^{-1/2} = 0.6065 $$ the origin of the factor 0.61 that appears in every wall-loss and Bohm-current estimate
The Shape of the Pre-Sheath Adding ion continuity with a volume ionization source, $d(nu)/dx = \nu_{iz} n$, and using the two relations above gives a closed form for the Mach number: the Tonks–Langmuir free-fall pre-sheath, and the curve plotted in the demo below: $$ \frac{x}{L} = \frac{3M - M^{3}}{2}, \qquad \chi = \frac{M^{2}}{2}, \qquad L = \frac{2}{3}\,\frac{\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c_s}}{\nu_{iz}} $$
The quasineutral solution ends in a singularity. Differentiating, $dM/dx \to \infty$ as $M \to 1$: the field the pre-sheath needs becomes infinite right at the sheath edge. That is not a failure of arithmetic, it is the pre-sheath telling us that quasineutrality has stopped being a valid approximation. What happens past that point is the sheath.
The two regions therefore live on completely different scales: the pre-sheath on the ionization or collision length, typically millimeters to centimeters, and the sheath on the Debye length, typically tens of microns. In the figure below the sheath has to be drawn magnified by a factor of tens just to be visible.
How Thick Is the Sheath?

Inside the sheath the electrons are all but gone, so the space charge is carried by the ion beam alone. That is precisely the Child–Langmuir space-charge-limited diode. Setting its current equal to the Bohm current fixes the gap:

Sheath Thickness $$ \boxed{\; s \;=\; \frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\lambda_{Ds}} \left(\frac{2\,\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\,|\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_0}|}{kT_e}\right)^{3/4} \;} $$

For a floating wall $\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}|\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_0}| \approx 5\,kT_e$, and the formula returns $s \approx 3\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\lambda_{Ds}}$, the rule of thumb that a sheath is “a few Debye lengths thick”. Bias the wall hard negative and it grows as $|\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_0}|^{3/4}$: this is the same law that sets the gap you need between accelerator grids, and the same law that limits the current density an ion engine can extract.

Show derivation
Child–Langmuir Current in the Gap A space-charge-limited flow of ions of mass $\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_i}$ across a gap $s$ held at $\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_0}$ carries $$ J \;=\; \frac{4}{9}\,\varepsilon_0 \sqrt{\frac{2\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_i}}}\, \frac{|\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_0}|^{3/2}}{s^{2}} $$
But the Plasma Decides the Current The sheath cannot draw more ions than the pre-sheath delivers, and the pre-sheath delivers the Bohm flux: $$ J \;=\; \textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\,\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_s}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c_s} $$
Equate and Solve for the Gap Every constant collapses into the Debye length at the sheath edge, $\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\lambda_{Ds}}^2 = \varepsilon_0 kT_e/\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}^2\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_s}$: $$ \boxed{\; s = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\lambda_{Ds}} \left(\frac{2\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}|\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_0}|}{kT_e}\right)^{3/4} \;} $$
The exact collisionless sheath solution actually approaches the sheath edge asymptotically, so “where the sheath begins” is a matter of convention rather than a sharp boundary. The Child–Langmuir estimate is the standard convention, and it is the one the demo reports.
Turned around, this is the perveance limit of an ion optics system: for a chosen grid gap and voltage there is a maximum extractable current density. Push past it and the sheath at the screen grid moves, the beamlet defocuses, and ions start hitting the accelerator grid.
Guiding Question

Inside the sheath, how do the ion and electron densities compare, and why?

Demo 3: The Full Structure

This is the whole picture at once: φ(x) above, the two densities below, the sheath edge marked by the dotted line, and the wall on the right. In the pre-sheath the two density curves lie on top of one another; past the sheath edge ne collapses while ni merely sags, and that gap is the space charge holding the potential down. Raise the density and watch the sheath thin while the drop stays put; raise Te and everything deepens together. Note the horizontal scale is split: the sheath is drawn magnified, and the panel tells you by how much.

None of this is bookkeeping. The sheath sets three numbers a thruster designer cannot escape. It fixes the ion current lost to the walls at $0.61\,\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\,\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_0} \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c_s}$ per unit area, and every one of those ions cost roughly 20–40 eV to make and produces no thrust. It fixes the energy each ion carries into the wall at about $5.8\,kT_e$, which for a Hall thruster running at $T_e \sim 25$ eV is roughly 150 eV: comfortably above the sputtering threshold of boron nitride, and the reason channel erosion is a life-limiting failure mode. And through the Child–Langmuir law it fixes the gap and voltage an ion optics system needs to extract a given beam current.

It also gives us our best diagnostic. A Langmuir probe is nothing more than a small wall we are allowed to bias: sweep it, and the exponential electron term traces out $T_e$, the saturated ion term gives $\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_0}$, and the zero crossing is the floating potential we derived above.

Conceptual Questions
If the plasma is quasineutral, how can there be a potential $\phi$ drop across it at all?
Quasineutral means $n_i - n_e$ is small compared with $n_e$, not that it is zero. Poisson's equation only needs a tiny fractional imbalance to sustain a field over a long distance. Across a pre-sheath of length $L$ the required imbalance is of order $(\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\lambda_D}/L)^2$, which for a 67 µm Debye length and a 10 mm pre-sheath is about one part in twenty thousand. The plasma is quasineutral to four decimal places and still supports a 2.5 V drop. The sheath is the opposite extreme: there the imbalance is of order unity, which is exactly why it can only be a few Debye lengths thick.
Why does the Bohm speed $c_s$ use the electron temperature and the ion mass?
Because the ions are not pushing themselves. In a typical discharge the ions are nearly cold (a fraction of an eV) while the electrons are at several eV. The electrons want to run to the wall and cannot, because that would leave a charge imbalance; instead they set up an ambipolar field that drags the ions along with them. So the energy scale of the motion comes from the electrons and the inertia comes from the ions. The combination $\sqrt{kT_e/\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_i}}$ is the ion-acoustic speed, the speed of sound in exactly this kind of two-temperature medium.
Why does a heavier propellant give a deeper sheath but a smaller wall loss?
The two effects come from the same mass, entering in different places. The floating potential depends on $\ln(\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_i})$, so a heavier ion deepens the well, but only logarithmically, which is why xenon and argon differ by less than 15%. The wall loss depends on $\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c_s} \propto \textcolor{#FF4400}{m_i}^{-1/2}$, which is a power law: xenon ions crawl to the wall about 2.8 times more slowly than argon ions at the same $T_e$, so the Bohm flux is 2.8 times smaller. Heavy propellant loses less plasma to the walls, and that is one of several reasons xenon is the standard choice.
The demo shows ni staying finite at the wall while ne goes to nothing. Shouldn't both fall to zero?
No, and the reason is a good check on the whole model. The ions are carrying a current to the wall, and current is $\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\,n_i u_i$. Since $u_i$ is finite, $n_i$ must be too, or no current would flow. Flux conservation gives $n_i = \textcolor{#C060FF}{n_s}(1 + 2\chi)^{-1/2}$: the ions thin out because they speed up, not because they disappear. The electrons are a different story: they are being turned back, so their density really does fall exponentially, by a factor of $e^{-5.3} \approx 1/200$ at a floating wall. Textbook sketches often draw both curves running into the axis; the physics says only one of them does.
What actually happens if you bias the wall positive instead?
The roles swap. Now the electrons are the attracted species and the ions are repelled, and an electron sheath forms with the opposite sign of space charge. Because electrons are so mobile, the surface draws an enormous current, roughly the 200× ratio we started with, which is why a positively biased probe can perturb or even extinguish the discharge it is measuring, and why a Langmuir probe sweep is normally kept close to the floating potential. It is also why the cathode keeper and the anode in a real thruster carry very different current densities for the same area.
Where does secondary electron emission fit in?
An electron or ion striking a surface can knock loose one or more secondaries, and those leave the wall: an outgoing negative current is indistinguishable from an incoming positive one. The wall therefore does not need to charge as deeply to reach balance, so the sheath gets shallower and the ions arrive with less energy. Boron nitride, the usual Hall thruster channel material, has a high yield, and above a critical $T_e$ the effective yield approaches one. At that point the sheath saturates: it can no longer deepen, the electron energy loss to the wall goes up sharply, and the electron temperature in the channel is effectively clamped. This is one of the main ways the wall material feeds back into thruster performance, and it is entirely outside the simple flux-balance model derived above.
How does a magnetic field $\vec{B}$ change the picture?
If the field is strong enough that the electron Larmor radius is smaller than the Debye length, the electrons can no longer stream freely to the wall: they have to cross field lines to do it, and their flux is reduced. The sheath then becomes shallower, and if the field meets the wall obliquely a second, thicker, quasineutral layer appears in front of the sheath: the magnetic presheath, or Chodura layer, whose scale is the ion Larmor radius rather than the Debye length. In a Hall thruster, where the radial field is designed to hold electrons off the walls, this is exactly the regime, and it is why magnetic shielding, shaping the field so its lines never terminate on the channel wall, so dramatically extends thruster life.
