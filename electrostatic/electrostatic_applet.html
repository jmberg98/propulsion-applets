To give a charged particle speed, we must apply an electric field $\vec{E}$ over a distance, creating a voltage difference $V$ which the particle accelerates through.2 Positively charged particles , whereas negatively charged particles . A ball rolling down a hill. The potential is the landscape, and the force $\vec{F} = q\vec{E}$ on a positive charge $q$ points downhill, from high $V$ to low $V$. The same hill, climbed. Flipping the sign of the charge $q$ flips the force $\vec{F} = q\vec{E}$, so a negative charge runs up the slope, from low $V$ to high $V$. Recall the common phrase “opposites attract, likes repel.” In doing so, we repel a positively charged particle away from our positively charged surface, creating thrust $F$! Guiding Question

Is it possible to exceed the specific impulse of a chemical rocket by accelerating a charged particle with an electric field $\vec{E}$? Recall that the theoretical maximum specific impulse is roughly 500 seconds, or 4905 m/s.

Conceptual Question
Why are we exploring both electric $\vec{E}$ and magnetic fields $\vec{B}$? If an electric field $\vec{E}$ is all that is needed to accelerate a particle to very high velocities, why are electric propulsion devices complex?
Electric $\vec{E}$ and magnetic fields $\vec{B}$ allow us to manipulate the trajectories of charged particles in more ways than simply “forward” or “backward.” Recall that the inert gas propellant of electric propulsion devices is not itself ionized. It must be ionized by other means, requiring radio-frequency (RF) devices or electron emitters, before it can be accelerated by a plate.

Additionally, the positively charged exhaust jet must be neutralized! Why? Recall that charge, like mass and energy, must be conserved. If a lot of positive charge $q$ leaves the spacecraft, the spacecraft is left charged highly negative, which can disrupt electronics and be catastrophic.5 Moreover, if the positive charge is not neutralized, it will eventually accelerate back to the negatively charged spacecraft, canceling the thrust $F$! These factors introduce engineering requirements of electric propulsion devices beyond an accelerating electric field $\vec{E}$.
Motivation for Electric Propulsion

Increasing effective exhaust velocity $c$, or specific impulse $I_{sp}$, is clearly the greatest driver for achieving high $\Delta V$ or meeting a $\Delta V$ specification using less propellant $m_{\mathrm{prop}}$. Assume we have a chemical engine that uses liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen as propellants, one of the most energetic reactions available to us. Assume that combustion is 100% efficient and that the engine nozzle is perfectly expanded such that exit pressure is 0 Pascals. These assumptions are intentionally optimistic. In making them, we derive the upper limit of the specific impulse for chemical engines, which is around 500 seconds.3,4 If we want to increase specific impulse further, we must decouple the energy source of the propulsion system from the propellant. If we instead apply a voltage $V$ to a charged propellant, we can clearly achieve much higher specific impulses, limited only by our choice of mass flow rate and power $P$. Electric propulsion aims to achieve much higher specific impulses than available by traditional chemical engines.1

Exhaust Velocity

The exhaust velocity $v_e$ of a charged particle with charge $q$ and mass $m$ after having an electric field $\vec{E}$ of magnitude $E$ over a distance $d$, or equivalently a voltage $V$ difference $\Delta V$ or $\phi$ applied is:

$$ \boxed{\; \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_e} = \sqrt{2\,\frac{\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m}}\,\Delta \textcolor{#F5C518}{V}} \;} \qquad \boxed{\; \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_e} = \sqrt{2\,\frac{\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m}}\,|\textcolor{#F5C518}{E}|\,d} \;} $$

Lorentz Force & Newton's Second Law $$ \vec{\textcolor{#FF4400}{F}} = \textcolor{#C060FF}{q}\!\left(\textcolor{#F5C518}{\vec{E}} + \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\vec{v}} \times \textcolor{#00DDB5}{\vec{B}}\right) \quad \vec{\textcolor{#FF4400}{F}} = \textcolor{#FF4400}{m}\,\frac{d\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\vec{v}}}{dt} $$ For $\textcolor{#00DDB5}{\vec{B}} = 0$ $$ \textcolor{#FF4400}{m}\,\frac{d\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\vec{v}}}{dt} = \textcolor{#C060FF}{q}\,\textcolor{#F5C518}{\vec{E}} \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{d\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\vec{v}}}{dt} = \frac{\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m}}\,\textcolor{#F5C518}{\vec{E}} $$ For Uniform, Constant $\textcolor{#F5C518}{\vec{E}}$ $$ \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v} = \frac{\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m}}\,|\textcolor{#F5C518}{E}|\,t \qquad x = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\frac{\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m}}\,|\textcolor{#F5C518}{E}|\,t^2 $$ Use Variables at Desired $v_e$ & Distance $d$ $$ \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_e} = \frac{\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m}}\,|\textcolor{#F5C518}{E}|\,t_e $$ $$ d = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\frac{\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m}}\,|\textcolor{#F5C518}{E}|\,t_e^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; t_e = \sqrt{2\,\frac{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m}\,d}{\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}\,|\textcolor{#F5C518}{E}|}} $$ Substitute $t_e$ $$ \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_e} = \frac{\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m}}\,|\textcolor{#F5C518}{E}|\sqrt{2\,\frac{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m}\,d}{\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}\,|\textcolor{#F5C518}{E}|}} = \sqrt{2\,\frac{\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m}}\,|\textcolor{#F5C518}{E}|\,d} $$
Via the Voltage
Apply a potential difference $V$ (or voltage) to a stationary particle with mass $m$ and charge $q$. The exhaust velocity $v_e$ is measured when $V_f = 0$. Energy Balance $$ KE_i + \text{PE}_i = KE_f + \text{PE}_f $$ $$ \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\textcolor{#FF4400}{m}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_i}^{2}}_{=\,0} + \textcolor{#C060FF}{q}\,\Delta \textcolor{#F5C518}{V} = \tfrac{1}{2}\textcolor{#FF4400}{m}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_e}^{2} + \underbrace{\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}\,\textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi_f}}_{=\,0} $$ Solve for $v_e$ $$ \textcolor{#C060FF}{q}\,\Delta \textcolor{#F5C518}{V} = \tfrac{1}{2}\textcolor{#FF4400}{m}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_e}^{2} $$ $$ \boxed{\; \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_e} = \sqrt{2\,\frac{\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m}}\,\Delta \textcolor{#F5C518}{V}} \;} $$
The two forms are equivalent: the field and voltage are related by $\Delta \textcolor{#F5C518}{V} = |\textcolor{#F5C518}{E}|\,d$, so $|\textcolor{#F5C518}{E}| = \dfrac{\Delta \textcolor{#F5C518}{V}}{d}$.
Electric Field & Potential

The electric field $\vec{E}$ and potential $\phi$ are related by a work-energy relation. For a given electric potential difference $V$ applied over a greater distance, there is a weaker electric field. Likewise, for a given electric potential difference applied over a smaller distance, there is a stronger electric field. Notice that the electric field lines point in the direction of decreasing electric potential $\phi$. This is very similar to gravitational potential energy, so think of . The same voltage drop $V$, spread two ways. Over a long distance the hill is gentle and the field $\vec{E}$ is weak; squeezed into a short distance the same drop makes a steep hill and a strong field. The ball rolls downhill either way, just harder on the steep one. We will return to these potential gradients when examining magnetic mirrors.

$$ \boxed{\; \textcolor{#F5C518}{\vec{E}} = -\nabla\textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi} = -\frac{d\textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi}}{dx}\ \text{(in 1D)} \;} $$

Connect the electric field $\vec{E}$ and electric potential $\phi$ or $V$ by energy balance. Work Done on Charge = Change in Electric Potential Energy $$ -\!\int \textcolor{#FF4400}{F_e}\,dx = \textcolor{#C060FF}{q}\,\textcolor{#F5C518}{V} $$ $$ -\!\int \textcolor{#C060FF}{q}\,\textcolor{#F5C518}{E}\,dx = \textcolor{#C060FF}{q}\,\textcolor{#F5C518}{V} $$ Use the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus $$ -\cancel{\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}}\,\textcolor{#F5C518}{E} = \cancel{\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}}\,\frac{d\textcolor{#F5C518}{V}}{dx} $$ Solve for $E$ $$ \boxed{\; \textcolor{#F5C518}{E} = -\frac{d\textcolor{#F5C518}{V}}{dx} = -\frac{d\textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi}}{dx} \;} $$
