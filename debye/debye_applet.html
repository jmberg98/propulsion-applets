What Is a Plasma? The Debye Length

Plasma Physics

Every electric propulsion device works on a plasma, so it is worth being precise about what that word means. Start with an ordinary gas and add enough energy to knock electrons off some of the atoms. What is left is an ionized gas: a mixture of neutral atoms that were never ionized, positively charged ions, and the negatively charged electrons that were freed along the way.

Color key ion, λD, ωp charge q, ne, power electron potential φ neutral mass

Ionization never produces a charge out of nothing: pulling one electron off a neutral atom leaves behind exactly one ion. So however hard we ionize the gas, the free positive and negative charges are created in matched pairs, and their number densities stay equal. That property has a name, quasineutrality, and it is the defining feature of a plasma.

$$ \textcolor{#4d9bff}{n_+} \;\approx\; \textcolor{#C060FF}{n_-} $$
Guiding Question

A neutral gas is heated until a third of its atoms have been ionized. How do the numbers of free positive and negative charges compare?

Demo 1: An Ionized Gas

Drag the ionization fraction from 0 % to 100 % and watch the census: the ion and electron counts move together, always. Notice too that the electrons dart around far more quickly than the ions: at the same temperature a xenon ion is about 240,000 times heavier, so it moves some 490 times more slowly. That speed difference is what makes the next section work.

Quasineutrality is not a law that is enforced everywhere at once: it is a result of the plasma constantly repairing itself. Suppose we drop an excess of positive charge into the middle of the plasma, or push a probe into it, or let a wall charge up. That charge sets up a potential. The light, mobile electrons are pulled inwards and crowd around it, while the ions are pushed away. Seen from far enough off, the cloud of extra electrons cancels the intruding charge completely: the potential is screened.

The screening is never perfect, because the electrons are also in thermal motion and refuse to sit still in the potential well. The competition between the electrostatic pull and the thermal energy sets a characteristic distance over which the potential survives before it is buried. That distance is the Debye length, λD.

The Debye Length

Balance the electrostatic energy of displacing the electrons over a distance λ against the thermal energy they carry, and the screening distance falls out directly:

Debye Length $$ \boxed{\; \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\lambda_D} \;=\; \sqrt{\dfrac{\varepsilon_0\, k T} {\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}^{2}\, \textcolor{#C060FF}{n_e}}} \;} $$ $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{F/m}$  ·  $k = 1.38\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K}$
Show derivation
Setup Take a slab of plasma and displace every electron in it by a distance $\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\lambda}$. That leaves a sheet of uncompensated positive charge behind and a sheet of negative charge ahead, of surface density $\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\,\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_e}\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\lambda}$. The work done against the resulting field, per electron, is $$ W \;=\; -\,\frac{\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}^{2}\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_e}}{\varepsilon_0}\, \frac{\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\lambda}^{2}}{2} $$
Balance Against Thermal Energy The electrons will keep pushing back out until the electrostatic energy they must climb equals the thermal energy they have to spend, one degree of freedom's worth, $\tfrac{1}{2}kT$: $$ |W| \;=\; \frac{\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}^{2}\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_e}}{2\varepsilon_0}\, \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\lambda}^{2} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,kT $$
Solve for the Screening Distance The factors of $\tfrac{1}{2}$ cancel, leaving $\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\lambda}^{2} = \varepsilon_0 kT / (\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}^2 \textcolor{#C060FF}{n_e})$: $$ \boxed{\; \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\lambda_D} \;=\; \sqrt{\dfrac{\varepsilon_0\, k T} {\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}^{2}\, \textcolor{#C060FF}{n_e}}} \;} $$
Hotter is longer. $\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\lambda_D} \propto \sqrt{T}$. Energetic electrons refuse to be held in the potential well, so the cloud they form is loose and spread out, and the charge reaches further.
Denser is shorter. $\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\lambda_D} \propto 1/\sqrt{\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_e}}$. With more electrons available, fewer of them have to move to cancel the same charge, so the job is finished closer in.
Solving Poisson's equation for the shielded charge rather than arguing by energy gives the same $\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\lambda_D}$, together with the shape of the decay: in one dimension the potential falls off as $\textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi(x)} = \textcolor{#F5C518}{\phi_0}\,e^{-|x|/\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\lambda_D}}$, which is the curve plotted in the demo below.
Guiding Question

You hold the electron density fixed and raise the electron temperature by a factor of four. What happens to the Debye length?

Demo 2: Screening a Charge

The horizontal axis is fixed at 700 µm across, so the curve is the thing that moves. Raise the temperature and the electron cloud loosens and spreads: the potential reaches further out. Raise the density and the cloud tightens until the potential is cut off almost at the source. The dashed markers sit at ±λD, where the potential has fallen to φ0/e of its peak.

The Debye length answers “how far?”. The companion question is “how fast?”. Displace the electrons in a slab of plasma bodily by a small distance $x$ and let go. The exposed sheets of charge pull them straight back, they overshoot, and the slab rings. That oscillation is the plasma frequency, and it is the rate at which a plasma repairs a charge imbalance.

The Plasma Frequency
Plasma Frequency $$ \boxed{\; \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\omega_p} \;=\; \sqrt{\dfrac{\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}^{2}\,\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_e}} {\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_e}\,\varepsilon_0}} \;} $$
Show derivation
Restoring Field Displacing the electron slab by $x$ exposes a surface charge $\sigma = \textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\,\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_e}x$ on each face, which sets up a uniform field $\textcolor{#F5C518}{E} = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$ pulling the electrons back. Newton's Second Law for the Slab $$ \textcolor{#FF4400}{m_e}\,\cancel{\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_e}}\,\frac{d^{2}x}{dt^{2}} \;=\; -\,\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\,\cancel{\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_e}} \left( \frac{\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\,\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_e}}{\varepsilon_0}\,x \right) $$ The density of oscillating electrons cancels from both sides, since every electron feels the same restoring field, so the frequency does not depend on how many of them are swinging together.
Simple Harmonic Motion $$ \frac{d^{2}x}{dt^{2}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}^{2}\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_e}} {\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_e}\varepsilon_0}}_{\textstyle \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\omega_p}^{2}}\, x \;=\; 0 $$ the equation of a harmonic oscillator, read off directly $$ \boxed{\; \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\omega_p} \;=\; \sqrt{\dfrac{\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}^{2}\,\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_e}} {\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_e}\,\varepsilon_0}} \;} $$
Numerically, $\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\omega_p} \approx 56.4\sqrt{\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_e}}$ rad/s with $\textcolor{#C060FF}{n_e}$ in m−3. An ion thruster discharge at $10^{17}$ m−3 rings at about 2.8 GHz, microwave frequencies, which is why plasma density is often measured with a microwave interferometer.
The two results are not independent. Multiplying them gives $\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\lambda_D}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\omega_p} = \sqrt{kT/\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_e}}$, the electron thermal speed. The Debye length is simply how far a typical electron travels in one plasma period: the plasma screens a charge in the time it takes its electrons to run across one Debye length.
Conceptual Questions
If a plasma has equal numbers of positive and negative charges, why say “quasi”neutral rather than just neutral?
Because the equality only holds when you average over a region larger than λD. Look closer than that, inside the shielding cloud around an ion, or within a Debye length of a wall, and the charge densities are genuinely different, which is exactly what lets a potential exist there at all. A plasma is neutral on scales large compared to λD and charged on scales small compared to it. That is what “quasi” is doing.
Why do the electrons do the shielding rather than the ions?
Both species respond, but not on the same clock. A xenon ion is about 240,000 times more massive than an electron, so at the same temperature it moves roughly 490 times more slowly. By the time the ions have begun to rearrange, the electrons have already been around the perturbation and back many times over. For anything that happens on the timescale of a discharge, the electrons have done the job on their own. The strict expression for the Debye length adds an ion term, but in the plasmas we care about it is a small correction.
It seems backwards that a hotter plasma shields worse. Shouldn’t more energetic electrons respond more strongly?
They respond faster, but shielding is not about speed: it is about being held in place. The cloud that cancels an intruding charge has to be a region where electrons pile up and stay piled up. Thermal motion works against that: a hot electron sails straight through the potential well instead of settling into it. The hotter the electrons, the deeper and wider a well has to be before it can hold them, so the potential reaches further before it is canceled. Raising ne has the opposite effect for the same reason: more electrons per unit volume means the pile-up needed is a smaller fractional change in density, achieved closer in.
Why does it matter that a device is much bigger than λD?
That comparison is what earns an ionized gas the name plasma. If the container is smaller than λD, the charges never get the chance to screen anything: the gas is just a collection of independent particles that happen to be charged, and the walls set the potential everywhere. If the container is much larger, the bulk of the gas is quasineutral and behaves collectively, and the charged regions are pushed into thin sheaths a few λD thick against the walls and grids. In an ion engine λD is tens of microns while the chamber is tens of centimeters, four orders of magnitude, so the plasma description is a very safe one.
What does the number of particles per Debye sphere tell us?
Shielding is a collective effect: it takes a crowd of electrons to bury a charge. The count $N_D = \textcolor{#C060FF}{n_e}\cdot\tfrac{4}{3}\pi\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{\lambda_D}^3$ checks that the crowd exists. If $N_D$ were of order one, the "cloud" would be a single electron and the statistical argument behind the Debye length would collapse. In the demo $N_D$ runs into the tens of thousands, which is why the smooth exponential curve is a fair description of what a real plasma does.
