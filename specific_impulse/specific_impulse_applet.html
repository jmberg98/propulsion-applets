Thrust & Specific Impulse

Mission Analysis

Propulsion systems expel propellant to generate thrust and ultimately change the momentum of the vehicle. They must be designed with key constraints in mind. Engineers must consider the mass budget and power budget, and satisfy the required mission time. These requirements dictate the exhaust velocity $v_e$, input power $P_{\mathrm{input}}$, and thrust $F_N$ of the propulsion system respectively.

Conceptual Questions
Why do high thrust propulsion systems tend to be less mass efficient? Likewise, why do more mass-efficient systems typically have lower thrust?
There is a limit to the amount of input power $P_{\mathrm{input}}$ available to accelerate the propellant, whether from chemical energy stored in the fuel or on-board solar/nuclear power. To achieve high thrust $F_N$, a large mass flow rate $\dot{m}$ of propellant must be accelerated, resulting in less power being delivered to each unit of propellant mass and thus a lower exhaust velocity $v_e$ and lower specific impulse $I_{sp}$. Conversely, to achieve high exhaust velocity and high specific impulse, the power must be distributed over a smaller mass flow rate, resulting in lower thrust.
How can high thrust and high specific impulse be achieved by the same propulsion system?
High thrust $F_N$ and high specific impulse $I_{sp}$ require a high power budget. There are drawbacks, however, to arbitrarily increasing input power $P_{\mathrm{input}}$. The amount of power that a power source can deliver scales with its mass. This can adversely affect the mass budget and mission time of the spacecraft. To effectively accomplish a higher power, higher power density is required. This makes nuclear power sources very appealing, as both fusion and fission can achieve much higher power densities than solar panels. Fission reactors have been flown in space, but fusion reactors (the holy grail) have yet to be realized.

Clarifying Points
Why do we care about mass efficiency? Can't we just carry more propellant or refuel?
In space, propellant is carried at a premium: every kg of propellant takes from the mass budget, leaving less mass available for payload and other important subsystems. Carrying extra propellant increases the mass of the vehicle, meaning the propulsion system must additionally handle the increased mass (more on this in the Ideal Rocket Equation!). Additionally, refueling introduces complexity and is not always possible, especially for deep-space missions.
Where does the input power for the propulsion system come from?
Ground-based propulsion systems exclusively rely on the stored chemical energy of rocket fuel and oxidizer for power to escape Earth's gravity. Once in space, other power sources become available. Spacecraft rely on solar or nuclear power to run their payloads, and leftover energy can be used to drive an electric propulsion system. This simulation assumes that input power $P_{\mathrm{input}}$ and mass flow rate $\dot{m}$ are decoupled, which is true for electric propulsion. Traditional chemical engines rely on combustion, so input power and mass flow rate are inherently coupled.
Thrust

A rocket carries its own propellant, so its thrust $F_N$ follows from conservation of mass and momentum across a control volume drawn around the engine. Assume steady state, no viscous forces, and a uniform exit stream of density $\rho_e$, velocity $v_e$, area $A_e$, and pressure $P_e$ discharging into ambient pressure $P_{amb}$. Let $\hat{\mathbf{i}}$ be the axial direction. The thrust stand holds the engine still, so what it reads is the reaction on the vehicle: the thrust.

Steady state · No viscous forces · Uniform exit stream along $+\hat{\mathbf{i}}$ · Propellant initially at rest on the vehicle
General Thrust Equation $$ \boxed{ \textcolor{#FF4400}{F_N} \;=\; \textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot{m}}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_e} \;+\; \bigl(\textcolor{#00DDB5}{P_e - P_{amb}}\bigr)A_e } $$ Effective Exhaust Velocity $$ \boxed{ \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c} \;\equiv\; \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_e} \;+\; \frac{\bigl(\textcolor{#00DDB5}{P_e - P_{amb}}\bigr)A_e}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot{m}}} } $$ $$ \boxed{ \textcolor{#FF4400}{F_N} \;=\; \textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot{m}}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c} } $$
Show derivation
Conservation of Mass $$ 0 = -\textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot{m}} + \rho_e \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_e} A_e $$ $$ \textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot{m}} = \rho_e \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_e} A_e $$ Reynolds Transport Theorem $$ \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\int_{CV}\!\rho\,\mathbf{V}\,dV \;+\; \oint_{CS}\!\rho\,\mathbf{V}\bigl(\mathbf{V}\cdot\hat{n}\bigr)\,dA \;=\; \sum\mathbf{F}_{ext} $$ Project onto the Axial Direction $$ \hat{\mathbf{i}}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\int_{CV}\!\rho\,\mathbf{V}\,dV \;+\; \hat{\mathbf{i}}\cdot\oint_{CS}\!\rho\,\mathbf{V}\bigl(\mathbf{V}\cdot\hat{n}\bigr)\,dA \;=\; \hat{\mathbf{i}}\cdot\sum\mathbf{F}_{ext} $$ Steady State $$ \hat{\mathbf{i}}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\int_{CV}\!\rho\,\mathbf{V}\,dV = 0 $$ $$ \hat{\mathbf{i}}\cdot\oint_{CS}\!\rho\,\mathbf{V}\bigl(\mathbf{V}\cdot\hat{n}\bigr)\,dA \;=\; \hat{\mathbf{i}}\cdot\sum\mathbf{F}_{ext} $$ Momentum Flux at the Exit Plane $$ \begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf{i}}\cdot\oint_{CS}\!\rho\,\mathbf{V}\bigl(\mathbf{V}\cdot\hat{n}\bigr)\,dA &= \rho_e \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_e^2} A_e \\[10pt] &= \bigl(\rho_e \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_e} A_e\bigr)\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_e} \\[10pt] &= \textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot{m}}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_e} \end{aligned} $$ External Forces $$ \hat{\mathbf{i}}\cdot\sum\mathbf{F}_{ext} \;=\; \textcolor{#FF4400}{F_N} + \bigl(\textcolor{#00DDB5}{P_{amb} - P_e}\bigr)A_e $$ Solve for Thrust $$ \textcolor{#FF4400}{F_N} + \bigl(\textcolor{#00DDB5}{P_{amb} - P_e}\bigr)A_e \;=\; \textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot{m}}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_e} $$ $$ \boxed{ \textcolor{#FF4400}{F_N} \;=\; \textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot{m}}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_e} \;+\; \bigl(\textcolor{#00DDB5}{P_e - P_{amb}}\bigr)A_e } $$
Specific Impulse

A propulsion system is designed to deliver impulse, a change in momentum, to the spacecraft. Propellant is the scarce resource in space, so the useful figure of merit is impulse per unit propellant mass $m_{\mathrm{prop}}$. Historically it was quoted per unit propellant weight on Earth, which is why specific impulse $I_{sp}$ carries units of seconds rather than m/s.

Specific Impulse $$ \boxed{ \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{I_{sp}} \;=\; \frac{\text{Impulse}}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_{\mathrm{prop}}}\,g_0} \;=\; \frac{\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c}}{g_0} } $$
Show derivation
Total Impulse $$ \text{Impulse} = \int \textcolor{#FF4400}{F_N}\,dt = \int \textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot{m}}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c}\,dt = \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c}\int \textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot{m}}\,dt = \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c}\,\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_{\mathrm{prop}}} $$ Normalize by Propellant Mass $$ \frac{\text{Impulse}}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_{\mathrm{prop}}}} \;=\; \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c} \qquad[\mathrm{m/s}] $$ Divide by Standard Gravity $$ \boxed{ \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{I_{sp}} \;=\; \frac{\text{Impulse}}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_{\mathrm{prop}}}\,g_0} \;=\; \frac{\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c}}{g_0} \qquad[\mathrm{s}] } $$
Input Power

In the spacecraft frame the propellant starts at rest and leaves at effective exhaust velocity $c$ relative to the spacecraft, so the jet carries kinetic energy away at a rate set by the input power $P_{\mathrm{input}}$ and the efficiency $\eta$ of the power conversion chain.

Power Balance $$ \boxed{ P_{\mathrm{jet}} = \textcolor{#C060FF}{\eta\,P_{\mathrm{input}}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \tfrac{1}{2}\,\textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot{m}}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c^2} = \textcolor{#C060FF}{\eta\,P_{\mathrm{input}}} } $$
Show derivation
Jet Kinetic Energy $$ E_{\mathrm{jet}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_{\mathrm{prop}}}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c^2} $$ Jet Power $$ P_{\mathrm{jet}} = \frac{dE_{\mathrm{jet}}}{dt} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot{m}}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c^2} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\textcolor{#FF4400}{F_N}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c} $$ Apply the Efficiency Factor $$ \boxed{ \tfrac{1}{2}\,\textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot{m}}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c^2} = \textcolor{#C060FF}{\eta\,P_{\mathrm{input}}} } $$
Exhaust Velocity $$ \boxed{ \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{2\,\textcolor{#C060FF}{\eta\,P_{\mathrm{input}}}}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot{m}}}} } $$ Thrust $$ \boxed{ \textcolor{#FF4400}{F_N} \;=\; \textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot{m}}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{c} \;=\; \sqrt{2\,\textcolor{#C060FF}{\eta\,P_{\mathrm{input}}}\,\textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot{m}}} } $$ Specific Impulse $$ \boxed{ \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{I_{sp}} \;=\; \frac{1}{g_0} \sqrt{\frac{2\,\textcolor{#C060FF}{\eta\,P_{\mathrm{input}}}}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot{m}}}} } $$

For a fixed input power $P_{\mathrm{input}}$, lowering the mass flow rate $\dot{m}$ raises exhaust velocity $v_e$ and specific impulse $I_{sp}$ but lowers thrust $F_N$. Designing a propulsion system around a power source is therefore a trade between the two.
