Hall Thruster II: Thrust Density & Electron Diffusion

Electric Propulsion

Unlike gridded ion engines, Hall Thrusters do not have a hard limit to the thrust $F$ they can produce for a given engine size. This means that Hall Thrusters have higher thrust densities $F/A$, making them attractive candidates for thruster miniaturization or for high thrust, high power missions.1,2,4

Experiment with the design voltages and other parameters to see the thrust densities $F/A$ associated with both gridded ion engines and Hall thrusters. It is not a hard rule that a Hall thruster will always have greater force density than a gridded ion engine, as it is dependent on these variables. However, because the grid gap distance can only be so small, the Hall thruster will always be able to achieve a higher thrust density with a higher Hall current.

Guiding Question

The accelerating voltage determines the specific impulse $I_{sp}$ of the thruster, but the Hall thruster curve tends to be greater than the gridded ion engine curve, indicating higher thrust density $F/A$ for the same specific impulse. What is the link between thrust $F$ and specific impulse $I_{sp}$ that Hall thrusters need in order to get anywhere near their maximum thrust densities?

In order to achieve high thrust $F$, high specific impulse $I_{sp}$ thrusters for say, human-crewed missions to Mars, a large amount of power $P$ is required. Solar power is limited by how much mass $m$ is required to generate the sufficient power for these devices. Nuclear power sources are the most promising due to their low specific mass.

However, another obstacle exists in developing high thrust $F$ Hall thruster engines. Recall from Hall Thruster I: The Hall Current, that particle collisions disrupt the Larmor motion of electrons, causing them to drift toward the anode at the bottom of the discharge channel. This is a loss, as an electron could have had a longer path length with more opportunities to ionize a neutral particle.

Guiding Question

Higher thrust $F$ necessitates higher mass flow $\dot{m}$ for a mission specified $\Delta V$ (and thus a specified $I_{sp}$). Why might higher mass flow reduce the efficiency of a Hall thruster?

Electron Diffusion

Though the radial magnetic field strength is sufficient to sustain magnetic mirroring, recall that magnetic mirrors rely on the magnetic moment being constant. If the cyclotron motion of the electron is disrupted by a collision, then it will stop being confined to the magnetic mirror. It will fall toward higher potential, closer to the anode, and gain velocity. After one Larmor radius, it gains enough velocity to start gyrating about the magnetic field lines, and is thus confined by the magnetic field lines. Likewise, another collision with a neutral will cause the electron to diffuse to the anode until it is collected.

The chart below asks the same question in the terms a designer actually budgets against: every electron that leaks to the anode this way is discharge current spent on nothing, so as propellant flow rises, the electron leak fraction rises with it and anode efficiency falls. The dashed red line marks the customary design target, a leak no worse than one-fifth of the beam current; drag the mass flow slider until the solid curve crosses it; and watch the status line below the sliders call out whether the current setting is inside or past that target.

Experiment with the variables and notice the speed at which the electron of interest drifts toward the anode. The setting includes an observed, not well understood mechanism that causes charged particle diffusion across magnetic field lines $\vec{B}$ to be faster than expected. Collisions alone let an electron hop about one Larmor radius $r_L$ across the field $\vec{B}$ per collision, which is slow. Real thrusters leak electrons to the anode far faster than that, so models carry an extra diffusion term in place of the missing physics. This is a challenge in both electric propulsion and fusion devices. For modeling purposes, it is treated as another diffusion component.5,6,7

Thrust Density

With no space-charge ceiling to run into, a Hall thruster's thrust density $F/A$ is set by whatever ion current density the discharge can sustain and by the voltage those ions fall through. There is no gap distance $d$ in it anywhere, which is the whole difference from the gridded engine.

Hall Thruster Thrust Density $$ \boxed{\;\frac{\textcolor{#FF4400}{F}}{A} = \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}\,\sqrt{\frac{2\,\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_i}\,\textcolor{#F5C518}{V}}{\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}}}\;} $$
Show derivation
Thrust from the Ion Mass Flow $$ \textcolor{#FF4400}{F} = \textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot{m}_i}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_i} $$ Exit Velocity of an Ion Born at Potential $\textcolor{#F5C518}{V}$ The ion is simply accelerated by the electrostatic field, so all of the potential $V$ it falls through becomes kinetic energy: $$ \tfrac{1}{2}\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_i}\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_i}^{2} = \textcolor{#C060FF}{q}\textcolor{#F5C518}{V} \;\;\rightarrow\;\; \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_i} = \sqrt{\frac{2\,\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}\,\textcolor{#F5C518}{V}}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_i}}} $$ Ion Mass Flow from the Beam Current Density Each ion carries one charge $\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}$ and one mass $\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_i}$, so a current density $\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}$ through an area $A$ is a mass flow of: $$ \textcolor{#FF4400}{\dot{m}_i} = \frac{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_i}}{\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}\,A $$ Combine and Divide by the Area $$ \frac{\textcolor{#FF4400}{F}}{A} = \frac{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_i}}{\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}}\,\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j} \sqrt{\frac{2\,\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}\,\textcolor{#F5C518}{V}}{\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_i}}} $$ $$ \boxed{\;\frac{\textcolor{#FF4400}{F}}{A} = \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}\,\sqrt{\frac{2\,\textcolor{#FF4400}{m_i}\,\textcolor{#F5C518}{V}}{\textcolor{#C060FF}{q}}}\;} $$
Nothing here caps $j$. Because the acceleration happens inside a quasineutral plasma, no space charge builds up to hold the next ion back, so the current density is limited by how much propellant is ionized rather than by the geometry. That is why the acceleration distance can be several centimeters instead of the $0.5$–$1\ \mathrm{mm}$ gap a gridded engine needs.3,4
Child–Langmuir Law

The current density $j$ in between the screen and accelerator grid of a gridded ion engine is limited by the Child–Langmuir Law. If too many positively charged ions exist between the two grids, the next ion to be extracted by the grid field $\vec{E}$ would be repelled by the amount of ions in between the grids. This is the ceiling the Hall thruster does not have.

Space-Charge-Limited Current Density $$ \boxed{\;\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j} = \frac{4}{9}\,\varepsilon_0 \sqrt{\frac{2q}{m}}\, \frac{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}^{3/2}}{d^{2}}\;} $$
Show derivation
Poisson’s Equation $$ \nabla^{2}\phi = \frac{d^{2}\phi}{dx^{2}} = -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} $$ Current Density $$ \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j} = \rho v \;\;\rightarrow\;\; \rho = \frac{\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}}{v} $$ Conservation of Energy $$ \tfrac{1}{2}m v_0^{2} + q\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T} = \tfrac{1}{2}m v^{2} + q\phi $$ $$ \rightarrow\; v = \sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}\left(\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi\right)} \;\;\rightarrow\;\; \rho = \frac{\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}}{\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}\left(\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi\right)}} $$ Substitute into Poisson’s Equation $$ \frac{d^{2}\phi}{dx^{2}} = -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} = -\frac{\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}}{\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}}}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi}} $$ Recall Chain + Power Rule $$ \frac{d}{dx}\!\left(\frac{d\phi}{dx}\right)^{2} = 2\,\frac{d\phi}{dx}\,\frac{d^{2}\phi}{dx^{2}} $$ $$ \rightarrow\; \frac{d}{dx}\!\left(\frac{d\phi}{dx}\right)^{2} = \frac{-2\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}\,\frac{d\phi}{dx}}{\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}}\,\sqrt{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi}} $$ $$ \rightarrow\; \int_{0}^{\frac{d\phi}{dx}} d\!\left(\frac{d\phi}{dx}\right)^{2} = \frac{-2\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}}{\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}}} \int_{V_T}^{\phi}\frac{d\phi'}{\sqrt{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi'}} $$

The field vanishes at the screen grid at space-charge saturation, so $\left.\tfrac{d\phi}{dx}\right|_{0} = 0$ with $\phi(0)=\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}$.

$$ \rightarrow\; \left(\frac{d\phi}{dx}\right)^{2} = \frac{-2\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}}{\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}}}\,(-2)\left(\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi\right)^{1/2} $$ $$ \rightarrow\; \frac{d\phi}{dx} = \sqrt{\frac{4\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}}{\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}}}} \left(\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi\right)^{1/4} $$ Separate & Integrate $$ \int_{V_T}^{\phi}\frac{d\phi'}{\left(\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi'\right)^{1/4}} = \sqrt{\frac{4\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}}{\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}}}}\int_{0}^{x}dx' $$ $$ \rightarrow\; -\frac{4}{3}\left(\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi\right)^{3/4}\Big|_{V_T}^{\phi} = \sqrt{\frac{4\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}}{\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}}}}\;x $$ Square Both Sides $$ \frac{16}{9}\left(\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi\right)^{3/2} = \frac{4\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j}}{\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}}}\,x^{2} $$ Rearrange for Current $$ \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j} = \frac{4}{9}\,\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}}\, \frac{\left(\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}-\phi\right)^{3/2}}{x^{2}} $$ Evaluate at $x=d,\;\phi=0$ $$ \boxed{\;\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{j} = \frac{4}{9}\,\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2q}{m}}\, \frac{\textcolor{#F5C518}{V_T}^{3/2}}{d^{2}}\;} $$
Electron Diffusion Across a Magnetic Field

Electrons are eventually all collected by the anode. The goal is to maximize their lifetime so that they have as many chances as possible to make an ionizing collision with a neutral on the way. The diffusion of electrons toward the anode is set by their cross-field diffusivity $D_\perp$. The Hall parameter indicates the path an electron takes after colliding with a heavy particle. A high Hall parameter means the path is highly curved (think $\vec{E}\times\vec{B}$ motion) whereas a low Hall parameter means the path is relatively straight (think straight toward the anode).3,4

Cross-Field Diffusivity and the Hall Parameter $$ \boxed{\;\textcolor{#ffe94d}{D_\perp} = \frac{\textcolor{#ffe94d}{D_e}}{1+\beta^{2}}\;} \qquad\qquad \boxed{\;\beta = \frac{\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\textcolor{#00DDB5}{B}}{m_e \nu_e} = \frac{\omega_{ce}}{\nu_e}\;} $$
Show derivation
Momentum Balance with No Magnetic Field Neglect electron inertia and ignore the electric field $\vec{E}$ (linear acceleration does not affect the conclusion), leaving the pressure $p_e$ gradient against collisional retardation: $$ \nabla p_e \;\approx\; -\,n_e m_e \nu_e \vec{v}_e \qquad \nu_e = \sum_j n_j \bar{c}_e Q_{ej} $$ Solve for the Electron Flux (Fick's Law) With $p_e = n_e kT_e$ and near-isothermal conditions, $\nabla p_e = kT_e \nabla n_e$: $$ n_e\vec{v}_e \;\approx\; -\frac{\nabla p_e}{m_e\nu_e} \;\approx\; -\frac{kT_e}{m_e\nu_e}\nabla n_e \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\;\textcolor{#ffe94d}{D_e} = \frac{kT_e}{m_e \nu_e}\;} $$
The collision frequency sits in the denominator. Collisions impede diffusion, so a more collisional plasma would give the longest electron lifetimes. However, this won't stay true when we apply a magnetic field.
Add a Magnetic Field Perpendicular to $\nabla p_e$ $$ kT_e\nabla n_e \;\approx\; -\,n_e m_e \nu_e \vec{v}_e - \textcolor{#C060FF}{e} n_e \vec{v}_e \times \textcolor{#00DDB5}{\vec{B}} $$ Cross the Whole Balance with $\textcolor{#00DDB5}{\vec{B}}$ One equation in two unknowns, $\vec{v}_e$ and $\vec{v}_e\times\vec{B}$, so a second is needed. Using the identity below, which holds because the flux being solved for is perpendicular to $\textcolor{#00DDB5}{\vec{B}}$: $$ \left[\vec{v}\times\vec{B}\times\vec{B}\right]_i = \epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{jlm} v_{el}B_m B_k = v_{ek}B_i B_k - v_{ei}B_k B_k = -\,v_{ei}\textcolor{#00DDB5}{B}^{2} $$ $$ kT_e\nabla n_e \times \textcolor{#00DDB5}{\vec{B}} \;\approx\; -\,n_e m_e \nu_e \vec{v}_e \times \textcolor{#00DDB5}{\vec{B}} + \textcolor{#C060FF}{e} n_e \vec{v}_e \textcolor{#00DDB5}{B}^{2} $$ Eliminate $\vec{v}_e\times\vec{B}$ Between the Two $$ n_e\vec{v}_e \;\approx\; \frac{-\dfrac{kT_e}{m_e\nu_e}\nabla n_e + \dfrac{\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\,kT_e}{m_e^{2}\nu_e^{2}}\nabla n_e \times \textcolor{#00DDB5}{\vec{B}}} {1+\left(\dfrac{\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\textcolor{#00DDB5}{B}}{m_e\nu_e}\right)^{2}} $$ Define the Hall Parameter $$ \beta = \frac{\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\textcolor{#00DDB5}{B}}{m_e\nu_e} = \frac{\omega_{ce}}{\nu_e} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad n_e\vec{v}_e \approx \frac{-\textcolor{#ffe94d}{D_e}\nabla n_e - \vec\beta \times \textcolor{#ffe94d}{D_e}\nabla n_e}{1+\beta^{2}} $$ The second term is perpendicular to both $\textcolor{#00DDB5}{\vec{B}}$ and $\nabla n_e$: it is the $\nabla p \times \vec{B}$ drift, which carries particles around the gradient rather than down it, and moves nothing toward the anode. The first term is ordinary diffusion, with: $$ \boxed{\;\textcolor{#ffe94d}{D_\perp} = \frac{\textcolor{#ffe94d}{D_e}}{1+\beta^{2}}\;} $$ Putting the electric field $\vec{E}$ back in is a substitution rather than a rederivation: replace $\nabla n_e$ with $\nabla n_e + \left(\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}n_e/kT_e\right)\textcolor{#F5C518}{\vec{E}}$ throughout, and the same denominator governs the conductivity.
The Strongly Magnetized Limit, $\beta \gg 1$ $$ \textcolor{#ffe94d}{D_\perp} \approx \frac{\textcolor{#ffe94d}{D_e}}{\beta^{2}} = \frac{kT_e}{m_e}\frac{\nu_e}{\omega_{ce}^{2}} = \frac{m_e kT_e}{\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}^{2}}\,\frac{\nu_e}{\textcolor{#00DDB5}{B}^{2}} $$
The collision frequency has moved to the numerator. Across a magnetic field, collisions no longer impede diffusion, they are the diffusion: a collisionless magnetized electron goes nowhere at all. And $\textcolor{#ffe94d}{D_\perp}$ scales as $\textcolor{#00DDB5}{B}^{-2}$, so raising the field confines electrons very strongly.
At $T_e = 4\ \mathrm{eV}$ with $\textcolor{#00DDB5}{B} = 100\ \mathrm{G} = 0.01\ \mathrm{T}$, typical densities give $\nu_e \sim 2.49\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ against $\omega_{ce}\sim1.76\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, so $\beta = 706 \gg 1$. The two diffusivities are then $\textcolor{#ffe94d}{D_e} = 2.83\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{m^{2}/s}$ against $\textcolor{#ffe94d}{D_\perp} = 0.57\ \mathrm{m^{2}/s}$. Since a diffusing substance spreads roughly as $x \sim 2\sqrt{Dt}$, spreading one centimeter takes $t \sim 4.5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{s}$.
Anomalous Electron Transport5,6 Electrons diffuse to the anode faster than predicted with cross-field diffusivity due to collisions. This is due to anomalous, or “Bohm,” diffusion. The reason appears to be the equivalent collisionality produced by scattering off small-scale plasma density fluctuations, which are almost always present. This is the same situation that has kept tokamaks from delivering fusion power. This factor can be treated as another diffusion component. See the empirical Bohm diffusion term and more generalized anomalous term: $$ D_\mathrm{Bohm} = \frac{kT_e}{16\,\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\textcolor{#00DDB5}{B}} \qquad\qquad D_\mathrm{anom} = \frac{kT_e}{c_B\,\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\textcolor{#00DDB5}{B}} \qquad (c_B \sim 16\ \text{to}\ 100) $$ Written as an Equivalent Collision Frequency $$ \frac{kT_e}{c_B \textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\textcolor{#00DDB5}{B}} = \frac{m_e kT_e}{\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}^{2}}\frac{\nu_\mathrm{anom}}{\textcolor{#00DDB5}{B}^{2}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\;\nu_\mathrm{anom} = \frac{\omega_{ce}}{c_B}\;} $$ So $c_B$ can be thought of as an anomalous Hall parameter. For modeling purposes one simply adds the two collision frequencies, $\nu_e + \nu_\mathrm{anom}$, and puts the sum into $\textcolor{#ffe94d}{D_\perp}$. Note that the anomalous term scales as $\textcolor{#00DDB5}{B}^{-1}$ rather than $\textcolor{#00DDB5}{B}^{-2}$; experiments confirm the $\textcolor{#00DDB5}{B}^{-1}$ slope but indicate a somewhat smaller magnitude than Bohm's coefficient.
