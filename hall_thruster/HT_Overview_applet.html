Hall Thruster: Overview

Electric Propulsion

Before we look into the physics, it is important to acknowledge the history under which electrostatic thrusters were developed. Nearly all electric propulsion devices used today are electrostatic devices, meaning they use solely electric fields to accelerate charged particles for thrust. During the Cold War, the United States and Soviet Union began research on two such electrostatic thrusters: the gridded ion engine and the Hall-Effect thruster. While the United States began early theoretical research on the Hall-Effect thruster, its intrinsic plasma instabilities dissuaded further development efforts. The United States would go on to focus on the development of the gridded ion engine, while the Soviet Union would continue to research the Hall-Effect thruster.3

Hall-Effect thrusters are the most used electric propulsion devices today, particularly in satellite constellations such as Starlink or Amazon LEO. Compared to gridded ion engines, Hall-Effect thrusters have a higher thrust density and lower specific impulse. Additionally, the Hall-Effect thruster's ionization and acceleration stages are coupled, occurring in the same stage of the thruster rather than in discrete areas. Most visibly distinct is the massive glowing ring of electrons at the end of the discharge channel instead of the grids found in gridded ion engines.

The Hall Current →

The most visually distinct feature of the Hall Thruster is also its namesake: the glowing ring of electrons, the electron “buzzsaw”, or the Hall current. A strong axial electric field accelerates ions in the discharge chamber while also collecting electrons emitted from the external cathode. A strong radial magnetic field increases the path length of electrons emitted from the external cathode. It does so by confining the electrons to magnetic mirrors. Because these fields are perpendicular to each other, the electrons start drifting in the E cross B direction, forming the Hall current!

Thrust Density & Electron Diffusion →

Unlike gridded ion engines, Hall Thrusters do not have a hard limit to the thrust they can produce for a given engine size. This means that they are viable options for both miniaturized thrusters and high power, high thrust engines. Active research is done in both of these areas. This makes Hall thrusters very appealing options for satellite constellations and, possibly, Mars missions. The greatest obstacles to increasing the thrust of Hall thrusters is electron diffusion and the political challenges in using nuclear power in space.

The Cathode →

The cathode serves two roles: it provides the required electron current to neutralize the outgoing beam of ions (just like the gridded ion engine), and ionizes the neutral propellant particles injected into the discharge channel. The cathode can be configured either externally, like a gridded ion engine, or it can be placed coaxially in the center of the thruster.

Potential & Field Distribution →

Unlike gridded ion engines, the exhaust velocities of the expelled ions are not uniform. This is because neutral particles are ionized at different parts along the discharge chamber. Particles ionized “earlier” experience a greater voltage drop and thus have a greater exhaust velocity. Because of this, the distribution of the electric potential and radial magnetic field strength through the thruster channel is important to thruster performance.

Guiding Question

Hall thrusters seem to be less complex component-wise than gridded ion engines. They also have no hard limit to their thrust densities, so why are gridded ion engines generally preferable for deep space probes like the Dawn mission?
