Hall Thruster I: The Electron Buzzsaw

Electric Propulsion

In the gridded ion engine, two metal grids held the accelerating field, and the electrons were kept out of the gap by the screen grid. A Hall thruster has no grids. Its anode sits in plain view at the base of an open channel, a couple of centimeters from the exit. Left alone, the electrons would run straight to it, the discharge would short out, and nothing would accelerate.

Color key thrust ion charge q, electron B field E field neutral

What stops them is a radial magnetic field laid across the channel. It grips the electrons and effectively ignores the much heavier ions, and the trapped electrons end up drifting around the annulus in a closed ring: the Hall current, or in the literature the closed drift that gives this thruster family its other name.2

On this page we will call that ring the buzzsaw, because an electron in it does not simply circle. It also bounces rapidly in and out along the field, so its path saws back and forth as it goes around, and every neutral atom heading down the channel has to pass through the blade. The name is ours, for teaching. If you go looking in the literature, look for closed drift and Hall current.

Guiding Question

There is no screen grid between the discharge and the anode. What keeps electrons from crossing the last two centimeters to the anode and shorting out the discharge?

Three Motions, One Electron

A single electron in the channel is doing three things at once, and each one has already had an applet of its own. It gyrates tightly about the radial field line. It slides along that line toward one wall, meets a stronger field near the pole piece, and reflects. And the crossed fields make the whole guiding center drift azimuthally.

What makes the picture tractable is that the three separate cleanly in both time and space.

1. Gyration about the field line $$ r_{ce} = \frac{m_e \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_\perp}}{\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\,\textcolor{#00DDB5}{B}} \qquad\qquad \tau_{c} = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_{ce}} = \frac{2\pi m_e}{\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\,\textcolor{#00DDB5}{B}} $$
2. Mirror bounce across the channel, inner pole to outer pole $$ \tau_{b} \approx \frac{2\,w}{\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_\parallel}} \qquad\qquad w = r_{out} - r_{in} $$
3. Azimuthal drift around the annulus $$ \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_{E\times B}} = \frac{\textcolor{#F5C518}{E}}{\textcolor{#00DDB5}{B}} \qquad\qquad \tau_{d} = \frac{2\pi r_{mid}}{\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_{E\times B}}} $$

Put the channel's own numbers in and the separation is stark. Taking the geometry of the model in this lesson (r from 38 to 62 millimeters, so a 24 millimeter channel), a peak field around 150 gauss, an electron temperature near 20 electronvolts and roughly 300 volts falling across the last 20 millimeters, the three come out near 1 millimeter and 2 nanoseconds, 24 millimeters and 18 nanoseconds, and 314 millimeters and 314 nanoseconds. Each stage is an order of magnitude slower and wider than the one before it.

That ordering is not a coincidence of this thruster. It is the guiding-center hierarchy, one timescale per adiabatic invariant, and a Hall thruster is an unusually clean place to see all three at once.

The Buzzsaw in Three Dimensions
If an electron bounces radially far faster than it travels around the annulus, then its path around the channel is not a circle. It is a sawtooth. So how many teeth does the blade have?
Counting the Teeth

Divide one lap by one bounce and the answer falls straight out of the two timescales above.

Radial bounces per trip around the annulus $$ \boxed{\;N = \frac{\tau_{d}}{\tau_{b}} = \frac{2\pi r_{mid}}{\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_{E\times B}}} \cdot \frac{\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_\parallel}}{2\,w}\;} $$

With the numbers quoted above, roughly 314 nanoseconds per lap against 18 nanoseconds per bounce, that is about 17 teeth per lap. The blade is a real object with a countable number of teeth, and the count moves when you move the field or the electron temperature.

One detail the flat picture hides: the electron is also creeping slowly toward the anode the whole time. The blade is not a flat disk but a slowly advancing serrated helix.

The Mirror Is Leaky

It is tempting to stop here and say the magnetic mirror keeps the electrons off the walls. Work out the loss cone, though, and that story does not close. If the radial field falls roughly as one over r across the gap, the ratio from the outer to the inner pole is only about 62 over 38, near 1.6, which puts the loss cone somewhere around 50 degrees and leaves a large fraction of a Maxwellian population outside it. Those electrons reach the wall on their first pass.

What finishes the job is the wall sheath. The channel wall charges negative with respect to the plasma, and the resulting field repels most of the electrons that the mirror failed to turn around. The honest division of labor is worth stating plainly: the magnetic field is what confines electrons axially, by magnetizing them; the sheath is what mostly confines them radially; and the mirror assists with the radial job without being sufficient for it.

Why the Blade Ionizes So Well

Now the payoff. An electron that crossed the channel in a straight line would travel 24 millimeters and be collected. Sawing its way around instead, it covers that distance once per tooth and keeps going for many laps before collisions work it across to the anode. The ionizing collision it needs becomes overwhelmingly likely somewhere along that path, which is why a channel a couple of centimeters deep can ionize nearly all the propellant fed into it.

Compare the gridded engine, which needed ring-cusp fields to reach roughly 80 percent utilization. The Hall thruster gets there and further with a much simpler magnetic circuit, because the closed drift is a far better path-lengthening trick than a cusp is.2

Where the Metaphor Breaks Down
A buzzsaw blade has rigid teeth in a fixed pattern. Real electrons do not. How far should the picture be pushed?
The teeth are a statistical envelope, not hardware. Every electron has its own pitch angle and its own energy, so the tooth count computed above is a typical value across a spread, and the electrons in the loss cone never complete a tooth at all. The ionizing is done disproportionately by the high-energy tail of the distribution, exactly as it was in the gridded engine, so the electrons that matter most for ionization are not the typical ones the blade is drawn from.
The picture says electrons cross to the anode by collisions. Measured Hall thrusters pass far more current across the field than collisions alone can explain. What is missing?
