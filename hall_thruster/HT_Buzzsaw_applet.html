Hall Thruster I: The Hall Current

Electric Propulsion

In the gridded ion engine, two metal grids held the accelerating field, and the electrons were kept out of the gap by the screen grid. A Hall thruster has no grids. Its anode sits in plain view at the base of an open channel, a couple of centimeters from the exit. Left alone, the electrons would run straight to it, the discharge would short out, and nothing would accelerate.

Color key thrust ion charge q, electron B field E field neutral

What stops them is a radial magnetic field laid across the channel. It grips the electrons and effectively ignores the much heavier ions, and the trapped electrons end up drifting around the annulus in a closed ring: the Hall current, or in the literature the closed drift that gives this thruster family its other name.2

On this page we will call that ring the buzzsaw, because an electron in it does not simply circle. It also bounces rapidly in and out along the field, so its path saws back and forth as it goes around, and every neutral atom heading down the channel has to pass through the blade. The name is ours, for teaching. If you go looking in the literature, look for closed drift and Hall current.

The Hardware the Blade Runs In

The cross-section turned on its side, exit plane at the top, with the magnetic circuit drawn on the iron that carries it. The loop is closed and almost all of it is metal: soft iron from the center stem out along the back plate, up one of the four outer legs and back in across the front pole, with a single short gap where it crosses the channel at the exit plane. That gap is the only part of the path that does any work. Everything else is there to get the flux around again as cheaply as possible, so that where the field does cross open space it crosses in one place, radially, exactly where the blade runs. The windings drive it, and the cross and dot marks on them read the usual way: current into the page, and out of it. There is nothing to set here. Guiding Question

There is no screen grid between the discharge and the anode. What keeps electrons from crossing the last two centimeters to the anode and shorting out the discharge?

Three Motions, One Electron

A single electron in the channel is doing three things at once, and each one has already had an applet of its own. It gyrates tightly about the radial field line. It slides along that line toward one wall, meets a stronger field near the pole piece, and reflects. And the crossed fields make the whole guiding center drift azimuthally.

What makes the picture tractable is that the three separate cleanly in both time and space.

1. Gyration about the field line $$ r_{ce} = \frac{m_e \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_\perp}}{\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\,\textcolor{#00DDB5}{B}} \qquad\qquad \tau_{c} = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_{ce}} = \frac{2\pi m_e}{\textcolor{#C060FF}{e}\,\textcolor{#00DDB5}{B}} $$
2. Mirror bounce across the channel, inner pole to outer pole $$ \tau_{b} \approx \frac{2\,w}{\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_\parallel}} \qquad\qquad w = r_{out} - r_{in} \qquad\qquad \mu = \frac{m_e \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_\perp}^{2}}{2\,\textcolor{#00DDB5}{B}} = \text{const} $$ The first expression is an estimate and a generous one. It has the electron cross the whole gap at its full parallel speed and turn instantly at the wall, when what actually turns it is the mirror force $-\mu\,\partial B/\partial s$, which slows it the whole way in and reverses it early. The figure below integrates the second expression instead.
3. Azimuthal drift around the annulus $$ \textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_{E\times B}} = \frac{\textcolor{#F5C518}{E}}{\textcolor{#00DDB5}{B}} \qquad\qquad \tau_{d} = \frac{2\pi r_{mid}}{\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_{E\times B}}} $$

Put the channel's own numbers in and the separation is stark. Taking the geometry of the model in this lesson (r from 38 to 62 millimeters, so a 24 millimeter channel), a field around 150 gauss at mid-channel, an electron carrying about 20 electronvolts and roughly 300 volts falling across the last 20 millimeters, the three come out near 0.9 millimeters and 2 nanoseconds, 17 millimeters and 41 nanoseconds, and 314 millimeters and 360 nanoseconds. Each stage is roughly an order of magnitude slower and wider than the one before it.

One quantity has to be named before those numbers mean anything, and the figure below makes it a slider: the pitch angle, which is how the electron's one speed divides between the $v_\perp$ that sets the gyroradius and the $v_\parallel$ that carries it along the field line. They are not independent, and an electron cannot have all of its energy in both. The numbers above are for 60 degrees, which is a typical value and, more to the point, is outside the loss cone, so it describes an electron the mirror can actually hold.

That ordering is not a coincidence of this thruster. It is the guiding-center hierarchy, one timescale per adiabatic invariant, and a Hall thruster is an unusually clean place to see all three at once.

If an electron bounces radially far faster than it travels around the annulus, then its path around the channel is not a circle. It is a sawtooth. So how many teeth does the blade have?
Counting the Teeth

Divide one lap by one bounce and the answer falls straight out of the two timescales above.

Radial bounces per trip around the annulus $$ \boxed{\;N = \frac{\tau_{d}}{\tau_{b}} = \frac{2\pi r_{mid}}{\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_{E\times B}}} \cdot \frac{\textcolor{#3DBFFF}{v_\parallel}}{2\,w}\;} $$

With the numbers quoted above, roughly 360 nanoseconds per lap against 41 nanoseconds per bounce, that is about nine teeth per lap. The blade is a real object with a countable number of teeth, and the count moves when you move the field, the electron's energy or its pitch angle.

It is worth knowing where the larger number you may have seen for this comes from. Estimate the bounce as $\tau_b \approx 2w/v_\parallel$ with the electron's whole speed taken as $v_\parallel$, and the count comes out nearer twenty. That estimate is not wrong arithmetic; it is a different electron. An electron with all of its energy along the field line has no $v_\perp$, therefore no magnetic moment to be pushed back by, therefore sits squarely in the loss cone and is on a wall before it has finished a lap. The electrons that stay are the ones carrying most of their energy across the field, and they cross the channel slowly. Fewer teeth, cut deeper, by an electron that is still there to cut them.

The figure below builds that blade one motion at a time, on the annulus it actually runs around. One detail it holds still for you: the electron is also creeping slowly toward the anode the whole time, so the real object is not a flat disk of teeth but a slowly advancing serrated helix. That axial creep is the subject of the next page.

Three motions, three orders of magnitude, one electron, drawn on the annulus it actually lives in. All three run to start with; switch them off in turn and the blade comes apart, switch them back on in order and it assembles. The bounce is integrated from the conservation of the magnetic moment rather than imposed as a triangle wave, so the electron slows as it climbs into the strong field at a pole and turns smoothly, and it turns early, before the wall, whenever the mirror is strong enough to turn it at all. Take the pitch angle below about 52 degrees and it stops turning early: that is the loss cone, and what sends the electron back is then the wall sheath, not the mirror. Every place the path reaches the ceramic is marked with a small red-orange ×, twice a bounce and all the way around, so an electron the mirror cannot hold is not merely labelled as such: you can see where it goes instead, and count the strikes. A confined electron puts none on the figure at all. The slider runs the full 0 to 90 degrees, so both edges of that argument can be looked at directly: at 90 the velocity is entirely across the field, the guiding center stands still at mid-channel and there is nothing left but the gyration, and at 0 it is entirely along the field, there is no magnetic moment for the mirror to push back on, and the electron runs flat into the ceramic. Push the pitch angle down toward zero and the teeth per lap climb back toward the twenty that the estimate $\tau_b \approx 2w/v_\parallel$ gives, because that estimate quietly assumes an electron with all of its energy along the field, which is exactly the electron the mirror cannot hold. Count the teeth off the path itself and the point of the whole figure is there to be counted: an electron that would have crossed the gap once in a straight line covers that distance many times over instead, and it has that many chances to find a neutral before anything collects it. The three cells under the figure are the numbers those teeth are cut from: the radius the electron gyrates on, the frequency it gyrates at, and the $\vec{E}\times\vec{B}$ drift that carries the whole orbit around the ring. Two things are drawn kindly rather than faithfully, and both are stated on the figure: the gyro-orbit is magnified, since at true scale it is under a millimeter on a thruster 180 millimeters across, and the discharge glow is held below the brightness a photograph would record, because at full strength it buries the path this figure exists to show. The blade is also pinned to the exit plane; in the hardware the electron is creeping slowly toward the anode the whole time, so the real object is a serrated helix.

Same field, same channel, one species held and the other ignored. Gyroradius is $r_c = m v_\perp / qB$, so it scales with $\sqrt{m}$ at a given energy and with $\sqrt{E}$ at a given mass. A xenon ion outweighs an electron by about 240,000, and it is falling through hundreds of volts rather than carrying tens of electronvolts of thermal energy, so its circle comes out meters across while the electron's is a fraction of a millimeter. Push the field as high as the slider goes and the ion still will not fit. That is what lets one field confine the electrons and leave the beam alone.

The Mirror Is Leaky

It is tempting to stop here and say the magnetic mirror keeps the electrons off the walls. Work out the loss cone, though, and that story does not close. If the radial field falls roughly as one over r across the gap, the ratio from the outer to the inner pole is only about 62 over 38, near 1.6, which puts the loss cone somewhere around 50 degrees and leaves a large fraction of a Maxwellian population inside it. Those are the electrons the mirror cannot turn, and they reach the wall on their first pass. Set the pitch angle in the figure above below 52 degrees and you are looking at one of them: the teeth run flat against the ceramic instead of turning short of it.

What finishes the job is the wall sheath. The channel wall charges negative with respect to the plasma, and the resulting field repels most of the electrons that the mirror failed to turn around. The honest division of labor is worth stating plainly: the magnetic field is what confines electrons axially, by magnetizing them; the sheath is what mostly confines them radially; and the mirror assists with the radial job without being sufficient for it.

Guiding Question

Besides the magnetic mirror many electrons are trapped by, what else keeps electrons from coming in contact with the discharge channel walls and losing their charge?

Why the Blade Ionizes So Well

Now the payoff. An electron that crossed the channel in a straight line would travel 24 millimeters and be collected. Sawing its way around instead, it covers that distance once per tooth and keeps going for many laps before collisions work it across to the anode. The ionizing collision it needs becomes overwhelmingly likely somewhere along that path, which is why a channel a couple of centimeters deep can ionize nearly all the propellant fed into it.

Compare the gridded engine, which needed ring-cusp fields to reach roughly 80 percent utilization. The Hall thruster gets there and further with a much simpler magnetic circuit, because the closed drift is a far better path-lengthening trick than a cusp is.2

Where the Metaphor Breaks Down
A buzzsaw blade has rigid teeth in a fixed pattern. Real electrons do not. How far should the picture be pushed?
The teeth are a statistical envelope, not hardware. Every electron has its own pitch angle and its own energy, so the tooth count computed above is a typical value across a spread, and the electrons in the loss cone never complete a tooth at all. The ionizing is done disproportionately by the high-energy tail of the distribution, exactly as it was in the gridded engine, so the electrons that matter most for ionization are not the typical ones the blade is drawn from.
The picture says electrons cross to the anode by collisions. Measured Hall thrusters pass far more current across the field than collisions alone can explain. What is missing?
