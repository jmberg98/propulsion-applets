Hall Thruster III: Neutralization

Electric Propulsion

The argument for neutralizing the beam is the same one the gridded engine made. Charge is conserved: expel positive ions and do nothing about it, and the spacecraft is left negatively charged, which endangers its electronics and eventually pulls the exhaust back, canceling thrust.

Color key thrust ion charge q, electron B field E field neutral

What is different here is the hardware doing it. The gridded engine carried a neutralizer cathode whose only job was the beam. A Hall thruster has one hollow cathode mounted outside the body, and it does two jobs at once: it supplies the electrons that stream back into the channel to sustain the discharge and feed the buzzsaw, and it supplies the electrons that neutralize the beam.1

Neutralization

The thruster from the side, locked, with the beam running to the right. The cathode outside the body feeds two streams at once: one downstream into the beam, one hooking back over the exit plane to sustain the discharge. The dashed box out in the plume is a real sample volume, counting the ions and electrons actually inside it. Cut the cathode and watch what the beam does to the spacecraft it just left.

One cathode, two destinations. So how does the emitted electron current divide between them, and what fixes the split?
Where the Electrons Go

Every ion that leaves takes one positive charge with it, so the same current has to leave as electrons: that is the beam-neutralizing share, $I_b$. The rest of the cathode's emission streams back upstream into the channel and is collected at the anode, which is the current the discharge supply actually reads. The cathode's total emission is the two together,

$$ I_c \;=\; I_b \;+\; I_{e,\text{back}} \;=\; I_d $$ Cathode emission equals the beam current plus the back-streaming current, and that sum is the discharge current.

The constraint that closes it is that the spacecraft draws no net current in steady state. Cut the cathode in the viewer above and the books stop closing: the ions keep leaving, nothing replaces the charge they carry away, and the negative charge left behind grows on the hull until it is pulling the beam back. That is the same failure the gridded engine had, reached by the same arithmetic.

Cathode Coupling Voltage

Getting electrons from the cathode into the plasma is not free. The cathode sits at a potential somewhat below the plasma it is feeding, and that difference, the cathode coupling voltage, is voltage the ions never get to fall through.1 It is small next to the discharge voltage but it is not negligible, and it is one reason the beam energy does not equal the discharge voltage.
